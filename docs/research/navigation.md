# Research note — goal-directed navigation for the utility robot

Status: describes the approach implemented in `src/swarm_field/swarm/provider.py`, `src/swarm_field/swarm/navigator.py` and `src/swarm_field/swarm/steering.py` (v1), the A.3 navigation seam that turns an objective's targets into a `Move(to=CellId)` action (the high-level world model has no turn/facing). This note covers only goal-directed navigation for the utility (killer) land robot, which must steer toward a sensed pest while staying on passable ground (it cannot cut through crops). Coverage planning for the explorer is a related but separate problem, sketched in "Coverage control". The design context lives in `docs/navigation-module.md`; the domain background in the literature review (§3.2).

## The problem

The robot gets a goal (a relative displacement to a pest, fused from radio readings) and must approach it on a partially known field: crops form obstacles it must walk around and the field edge must not be crossed. It knows its own cell but has no trusted map — it probes which of its adjacent cells are clear to enter. In one tick it may step one cell into a clear neighbour (`Move(to=CellId)`) or stand still. The questions are: which adjacent cell leads toward the goal, and what to do when it is blocked.

## Candidate approaches

Artifical potential fields are the classic reactive answer (Khatib 1986): an attractive field pulls toward the goal, repulsive fields push off obstacles, and the robot follows the gradient. It is cheap, smooth and entirely local, which fits a swarm of resource-limited robots. Its famous failure mode is local minima: fields cancel on concave features, so a robot can circle a pocket forever (Borenstein & Koren 1989). Fields also need tuning (gains, influence radii) to behave near corners, and they are brittle on a discrete grid where a "force" can point into a crop cell.

Wall-following / Bug algorithms (Lumelsky & Stepanov 1987) are the other classical escape: when the direct path is blocked, hug the obstacle's boundary until the straight line to the goal becomes clear again. They are deterministic and need no representation of the world, just local touch; on a known-connectivity crop field, sliding along a row edge naturally deposits the robot at the row end, which is exactly where the aisle becomes reachable.

The Vector Field Histogram family (Borenstein & Koren 1991; Ulrich & Borenstein 1998) builds a polar histogram of obstacle density from range sensors and steers to the valley closest to the goal. It is a good middle ground but assumes a multi-direction rangefinder and, again, a continuous metric space; its goal alignment can also pick a corridor that dead-ends. Global planners (A* / Dijkstra / wavefront) provably find a route, and with a quadtree over the robot's own memory the cost is a bounded window rather than the whole field — but they need a map of free space, which the robot only partly has (belief is partial and noisy, per `docs/navigation-module.md` §3.1). That is why the planner here is in front of the reactive policy rather than beside it: it is fed by what memory knows, and the policy covers the ground it cannot see.

## Coverage control (why the explorer is a different problem)

Coverage control (Cortés, Martínez & Bullo 2004) drives a team to spread so that a density function over the field is optimally approximated, typically via Lloyd/Voronoi iteration over the robots' sensing footprints. That fits the explorer's job (watch the whole field) but is the wrong tool for the utility robot, which has a definite goal to run down. The two policies are complementary: the explorer keeps the field covered; when it broadcasts a found pest, the utility robot's navigator takes over to steer there.

## The chosen approach

Two layers behind one seam, `Navigator.step_towards(seeded, robot) -> Move | Idle` along the field `FieldProvider.field_for(seeder, robot, now)` built from a seeder's targets, ordered by what they can do rather than by how far away the goal is: the potential field guides whenever it can, and the reactive wall-follow is the fallback.

**First the field.** The objective's targets are seeded into a Dijkstra potential field over the quadtree of the robot's believed terrain, and the robot steps into the neighbouring cell the field points at. A field is better than a wall-follow at *every* distance, not only far away: it is the only one of the two that can see around a corner, so it needs no proximity test to trigger. Wall-following is a policy for a robot that has nothing else, which is a rare robot in this swarm. The field is the planner; the quadtree bounds its search to the robot's own 16 × 16 block and its neighbours, so a rebuild stays cheap, and it is reused while the targets, the terrain and that block are unchanged. What it cannot do is tell the robot to go *around* a field it has never seen, or through ground it believes is clear and is not.

**Then the wall-follow.** When the field cannot guide, the same reactive policy described in `docs/navigation-module.md` takes over: gradient alignment for the direct approach (pick the compass heading whose unit offset has the largest dot product with the goal direction, ties broken deterministically by enum order) and, when that heading is blocked, a committed right-hand wall-follow — pick the most goal-aligned *clear* heading once (ties broken clockwise from the goal heading) and stick to it for the whole episode. This commitment is the load-bearing choice: re-deriving the tangent against the goal bearing every tick is what traps a robot between two clear neighbours on either side of a crop row, shuttling forever (a pure greedy potential-field local minimum). The robot only re-routes when the wall itself forces it: a blocked tangent ahead rounds the corner toward the goal if that way is open, otherwise it shifts clockwise to keep hugging the wall. It breaks off the wall only once the goal is genuinely reachable — right next to it, or level along the slide axis with a clear bearing — which lets an east-west crop row be rounded cleanly at its headland end. When no route exists at all (all neighbours blocked, or the goal distance never beating its running minimum for a long stretch) it stops and reports `is_stalled`, handing the decision back to the brain (A.1), which may re-issue or replan the goal; it does not invent one itself.

There are exactly two ways into the fallback: the field has no target to offer (no wave reaches this robot, so the objective's target is unreachable on believed terrain — the steerer walks towards it anyway and stalls if it is walled off for good), or the field stopped guiding. The second is the one that needs a rule: route lengths through leaves are approximate, so a robot near the edge of its window can in principle turn back and forth over a lower estimate it has already beaten. The navigator watches the potential at the robot's cell and counts ticks without a new minimum; three is enough to hand the step to the steerer, and the handover is not latched — the field is asked again at the next decision point, so the robot returns to the route the moment the field points forward.

This choice is the low-risk, hard-to-misbehave option for the stated constraints: the field is deterministic (no RNG) and near-optimal over what the robot knows, the fallback is cheap, fully deterministic, and — because the detour is committed wall-following plus a fixed hand — free of both potential-field minima on concave corners and of the oscillation of naive "try the goal, else random step" policies. Its known limitations are accepted for v1 and recorded as open decisions in `docs/navigation-module.md` §13: the field is planned over *believed* terrain, so a wrong obstacle belief is followed exactly (memory has no re-observation rule that could pull a robot out of it); the fallback has no memory of visited cells, so a long dead end is only detected by the stall timeout (a tunable `stuck_ticks`); and there is no guarantee of a route where a global planner over ground truth would find one.
